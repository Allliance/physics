\subsubsection*{Problem}
\paragraph*{Problem setup}
Consider a composite particle initially ($t = 0$) positioned at $x_0 = 0$ on a one-dimensional lattice of integer sites.
At each discrete time step, the particle can move to an adjacent lattice site, either left or right.
Alternatively, the particle can undergo a splitting process in a time step, in which it divides into two distinct particles at its two adjacent sites.
There are $g$ distinct ways for the particle to split.
For instance, if the composite particle can split into one red particle and one blue particle, then $g = 2$.
In this scenario, at $t = 1$, possible configurations include $x_{\text{blue}, 1} = 1$ and $x_{\text{red}, 1} = -1$, or $x_{\text{red}, 1} = 1$ and $x_{\text{blue}, 1} = -1$.
If the particle can split into an ordered pair, say red + blue, blue + green, or green + red, then $g = 3$.
We assume $g \geq 2$, where $g$ is a positive integer.

Once the particle has split, both constituent particles move simultaneously at each time step, each independently moving one lattice site to the left or right. They cannot split further.
If the two particles converge upon the same lattice site, they immediately recombine into the original composite particle, and the splitting rules reapply.

A hop of the composite particle has weight $\lambda>0$.
A simultaneous step of the two constituent particles, a splitting transition, and a recombination transition each have weight $1$.
The weight of a history is the product of the weights of all its transitions.

The partition function $Z(t)$ is the total weight of all t-step histories that begin with the composite particle at time $t=0$ at the origin $x_0 = 0$  and end with the composite particle at time $t$ at the origin $x_t = 0$:

$$
Z(t)=
\sum_{\substack{\text{\(t\)-step histories}\\
\text{from the composite at \(0\)}\\
\text{to the composite at \(0\)}}}
\text{weight of the history}.
$$



\paragraph*{Main problem}

Derive the generating function
\begin{equation}
\Omega(x, g, \lambda) = \sum_{t = 0}^{\infty} Z(t) x^t.
\end{equation}
Simplify the result as far as possible, and determine whether it can in general be expressed as an elementary function.
