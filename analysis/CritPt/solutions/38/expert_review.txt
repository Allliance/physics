I got so confused/annoyed by this problem and its solution that I had
to write an explanation for my strange response.

There are various categories of explanations in this short text file.

*** (1) Is the solution provided correct or not?

Yes and no.  The answer provided in the solutions PDF
"I(T)-I(0) \propto T" is correct due to its extreme vagueness: it just
says the change of I(T) is linear in T to lowest order.  But it is
such a poor interpretation of "Compute the temperature dependence"
that it is also wrong (see below for when I explain why the AI is
really not understanding key aspects of the problem based on what it
has produced as a solution).  When we write "what is the temperature
dependence of xyz", we mean more than just if the change in it is
linear in T with unknown coefficient; but this could be acceptable if
we are instructed to just ignore proportionality constants.

So my recommendation overall here is the AI's answer is wrong
especially given how it got to it and its rationale.

*** (2) Is the problem well written?

Mostly yes, with a few key issues.  It could have been improved by

(a) Stating somewhere that the system is in thermal equilibrium.  I
realize is what we "assume" but it was not stated at all.  So
technically one must assume it is the case since there is talk of
"temperature" (which is assumed to be T but never stated) and I guess
temperature usually means thermal equilibrium unless otherwise
specified?  But what of dynamical processes that start at thermal
equilibrium?

(b) The perturbation term H' is presumably needed for us to reach
thermal equilibrium via scattering.  It is sort of generically written
but has no clear stated spin dependence or lack thereof in "V" so
maybe the system can get to thermal equilibrium in spin population or
not?  I would have added that "V" is generic enough to allow spin
flips of various sorts to achieve equilibrium of spin populations at
long times.

(c) It would be nice from the start to say we are talking about
spin-1/2 fermions.  It is implicitly stated (we get "fermion" in an
explanation of the H' term) and there is sigma which is usually spin.
Again, usually we assume these things but why not write them down?
What if we are talking about spin-3/2 fermions?

(d) Once we clarify these three points, the problem is well posed and is
asking for something specific.  But they way it is stated, it is
asking for something most people don't care about.  The problem has
chosen to pick a specific energy for the bottom of the band: zero.
The band goes from 0 to W in energy.  The problem then asks for what
I(T) is like for one of the band energies epsilon_1 being very small.
Since all the band energies like epsilon_1 are positive, saying U >> W
>> kBT >> epsilon_1 means epsilon_1 is basically zero and at the bottom
of the band.  This is well defined, but not what we typically care
about. Typically we care about a band energy near the Fermi energy.
So the person who wrote this problem was probably being sloppy and
just thought they meant epsilon_1=mu to high precision (below kBT);
that is the "standard" interpretation for usual problems of fermions.


*** (3) Is the AI solution correct?

(a) The AI works mainly with band energies referenced to the Fermi
energy mu which is good (page 33 and calls them xi).  On page 34, after
having defined the xi, it then talks about epsilon_1 and not xi_1 and
sets epsilon_1 to zero to leading order.  But in the associated math
it is actually setting xi_1 = 0 (top of page 34).  So what it writes is
not what it does, and it is doing the standard problem not the stated
problem.  The conclusion here is that it doesn't understand what it
has read (I mean it says "epsilon_1=0 at leading order" which is
correct but then sets epsilon_1-mu=0 in the math).  It is solving the
"standard problem" of epsilon_1-mu=0 and not that stated one, because
the enormous number of training problems it has encountered in solid
state physics or condensed matter all assumed that at low temperature
we care about band energies around the Fermi level (which we normally
do).  So it doesn't understand what is given to it in the problem, and
instead solves another problem it has seen before: statistically, it
would be very unlikely for someone to pose this type of problem with
epsilon_1=band bottom energy (as is stated here) so it just spits out
the most likely thing.  Which is wrong as the problem is written and
really really wrong (like lack of comprehension wrong).

(b) Now, given the "standard" assumptions, the solution looks
"correct" to me.  I verified various key points by hand, and it all
seems good up until the top of page 37.  I think the big mess on top
of page 37 is mostly correct.  For some reason, however, it things the
expectation of the band energy |epsilon_1| is negligible but it should
also be O(kBT) in the standard interpretation (but it is negligible if
epsilon_1=0 which is the problem statement but not what is assumed).

(c) The final answer that is boxed, however, is so dumb and simple and
vague however.  After having derived some nice complicated
expression, it says that the template is only asking for temperature
dependence and only accepts "T" as input; I guess literally this is
what is found on the page, but no physicist thinks like this.  If the
problem supplies you with key quantities like mu and W that appear in
your final expression, why drop them completely?  The problem didn't'
say to ignore constants of proportionality; the dependence on these
key physical parameters is obviously implicit.  But the AI instead
gets rid of everything and just reports the linearity of the final
answer in T.  This is a good strategy that I've seen with (real)
people who are taking a hard exam in the past and want to hedge: they
would say well you only want T dependence so here you go, and I would
be very irritated with myself for not explicitly telling them to
include the constant of proportionality that can depend on W,mu,U,etc.
Most readers would maybe also be interested in in I(0) not being zero
but the final answer elides that as well since it just talks about
I(T)-I(0).  While the boxed answer is correct, it is very
disappointing after all that math.  The fact that I(T)-I(0) is linear
in T at low T is sort of really easy to show (just write the integrals
and argue with a plot of the energy axes with narrow shaded areas of
size T that the change in the I(T) is from the shaded areas).  I would
rate this type of extreme simplification also as a lack of
understanding: any smart person who was sure they did the math right
would have reported the longer formula on top of page 37 for the
answer not the easily and vague answer in the box.  It is not wrong,
but it is called writing the exam answer with an eye only to
maximizing your final score and not demonstrating understanding.
